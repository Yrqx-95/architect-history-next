# Active Learning Examples

Generated: 2026-06-09

Scope: active-learning prompts for selected building pages. No database changes, no taxonomy changes, no metadata changes.

Goal: move from passive reading to active observation, comparison, and judgment. Each prompt asks the learner to produce an answer, not only absorb information.

## How To Use

On a building page, this layer should appear after the basic facts and before longer description. The user should be asked to write, choose, sketch mentally, compare, or make a defensible claim.

Each building uses five parts:

1. 30-second observation task
2. 3-minute analysis task
3. Common misunderstanding
4. Compare with
5. Studio Critique Prompt

## 1. Church of the Light

Architect: Tadao Ando
Current page slug: `church-of-light`

### 30-Second Observation Task

Look only at the main interior image or facade image for 30 seconds.

Write down three things:

- Where is the strongest contrast?
- Which direction does your eye move first?
- What is missing that you would normally expect in a church?

Do not describe the building as "simple" yet. Name visible decisions.

### 3-Minute Analysis Task

Make a claim:

> The power of this building comes more from light, material, geometry, or religious symbolism.

Choose one. Then support it with three observations from the image or plan.

Your answer should include:

- One spatial observation
- One material observation
- One emotional or bodily effect

### Common Misunderstanding

Misunderstanding: "The cross-shaped light is the whole idea."

Correction: The cross matters because the room is otherwise restrained. The concrete, darkness, seating axis, wall thickness, and lack of ornament all make the light perform. The lesson is not only the cross; it is the control system around the cross.

### Compare With

- Chapel of Notre Dame du Haut: compare sacred light as sculptural volume versus sacred light as precise cut.
- MIT Chapel: compare inward darkness and focused light.
- Water Temple: compare direct visual symbol versus ritual sequence.

### Studio Critique Prompt

If you removed the cross-shaped opening and replaced it with a plain vertical slit, would the building still work?

Argue yes or no. Your critique must address:

- Spatial orientation
- Religious meaning
- The role of restraint
- Whether the architecture depends on one image too much

## 2. Villa Savoye

Architect: Le Corbusier
Current page slug: `villa-savoye`

### 30-Second Observation Task

Look at the exterior for 30 seconds.

Answer quickly:

- Which parts seem to touch the ground?
- Which parts seem to float?
- Where do you think movement enters the building?

Then choose one word: object, machine, promenade, or landscape. Pick the word that best explains your first impression.

### 3-Minute Analysis Task

Trace an imagined route from arrival to roof terrace.

Write a five-step sequence:

1. Approach
2. Entry
3. Ramp or stair
4. Main living level
5. Roof garden

For each step, write what the building makes you notice: ground, column, window, wall, view, sky, car, or body movement.

### Common Misunderstanding

Misunderstanding: "Villa Savoye is important because it demonstrates the Five Points."

Correction: The Five Points are useful, but the building is not only a checklist. Its deeper lesson is how a house becomes an architectural route: arrival, pilotis, ramp, horizontal window, roof garden, and view form one continuous argument about modern life.

### Compare With

- Row House in Sumiyoshi: compare controlled movement in an open courtyard versus controlled movement in a modern promenade.
- Farnsworth House: compare floating modern object versus routed modern object.
- Unité d'Habitation: compare private villa principles with collective housing.

### Studio Critique Prompt

Is Villa Savoye more convincing as a house or as a manifesto?

Choose one position. Defend it with:

- One observation about daily life
- One observation about form
- One observation about circulation
- One criticism of its usability

## 3. Kimbell Art Museum

Architect: Louis Kahn
Current page slug: `kimbell-art-museum`

### 30-Second Observation Task

Look at an interior gallery image.

Write down:

- Where does the light come from?
- What repeated shape organizes the room?
- Does the space feel heavy, light, calm, or ceremonial?

You must choose only one mood word and justify it with one visual clue.

### 3-Minute Analysis Task

Compare structure and light.

Make two columns:

| Structure | Light |
|---|---|
| What repeats? | Where is light softened? |
| What carries weight? | Where does light touch the art? |
| What feels permanent? | What changes during the day? |

Then answer:

> Is the building primarily a container for art, or is it itself a lesson in seeing?

### Common Misunderstanding

Misunderstanding: "The Kimbell is quiet, so there is not much to analyze."

Correction: Its quietness is the result of exact decisions: vault rhythm, daylight control, material weight, gallery scale, and the relationship between structure and viewing. The calm is constructed.

### Compare With

- Salk Institute: compare repeated monumental order in a research campus and a museum.
- Louvre-Lens: compare soft museum neutrality versus Kahn's structured calm.
- Chichu Art Museum: compare daylight as controlled atmosphere.

### Studio Critique Prompt

Should a museum background disappear, or should it teach visitors how to look?

Use Kimbell as your main example. Your critique must include:

- How daylight changes the viewing experience
- Whether repetition helps or limits attention
- Whether the architecture competes with art
- One comparison with another museum

## 4. Rolex Learning Center

Architect: SANAA
Current page slug: `rolex-learning-center`

### 30-Second Observation Task

Look at the plan or interior image for 30 seconds.

Mark mentally:

- Where would you sit alone?
- Where would you meet a group?
- Where would you get lost?

Then decide: is this building more like a room, a landscape, or a campus?

### 3-Minute Analysis Task

Describe how the building organizes learning without conventional corridors.

Answer in three parts:

- How does the floor shape guide movement?
- How do open zones create or reduce privacy?
- What is the tradeoff between freedom and clarity?

End with a judgment:

> This building makes learning feel more open, more confusing, or more social.

Choose one and defend it.

### Common Misunderstanding

Misunderstanding: "The Rolex Learning Center is just an open-plan building."

Correction: It is not only open; it is topographical. Slopes, voids, low partitions, furniture zones, and sightlines produce different degrees of connection. The question is not openness alone, but how openness is graded.

### Compare With

- 21st Century Museum Kanazawa: compare field-like plan and non-hierarchical circulation.
- Seattle Central Library: compare free learning landscape versus highly structured information machine.
- Villa Savoye: compare architectural promenade as route versus continuous field.

### Studio Critique Prompt

Does freedom of movement improve learning, or does it weaken focus?

Use the Rolex Learning Center to argue one side. Your critique must address:

- Orientation
- Noise and privacy
- Social learning
- Whether plan flexibility becomes spatial ambiguity

## 5. 21st Century Museum Kanazawa

Architect: SANAA
Current page slug: `kanazawa-museum`

### 30-Second Observation Task

Look at the plan or exterior image for 30 seconds.

Answer:

- Where is the front?
- Where is the back?
- Which path seems most official?

If you cannot answer clearly, write why. The uncertainty is part of the exercise.

### 3-Minute Analysis Task

Analyze the circular plan as a public device.

Write three claims:

- One claim about access
- One claim about exhibition rooms
- One claim about the museum's relationship to the city

Then decide:

> Is this museum more democratic, more disorienting, or more flexible?

Choose one and support it with plan-based evidence.

### Common Misunderstanding

Misunderstanding: "The circular form is just a visual icon."

Correction: The circle changes how the institution meets the city. Multiple entries, transparent edges, distributed galleries, and non-frontal circulation make the museum feel less like a monument and more like a public field.

### Compare With

- Rolex Learning Center: compare circular public field with topographical learning field.
- New Museum: compare horizontal openness with vertical stacked galleries.
- Kimbell Art Museum: compare non-hierarchical circulation with ordered gallery rhythm.

### Studio Critique Prompt

Can a museum be too open?

Use Kanazawa as your case. Your critique must include:

- Whether multiple entrances help public access
- Whether gallery identity becomes weaker
- How transparency affects civic presence
- One comparison with a more directional museum

## Best Use On Building Pages

These prompts should not be hidden inside long prose. They work best as compact active-learning blocks:

- First block: "Look first"
- Second block: "Make a claim"
- Third block: "Avoid this mistake"
- Fourth block: "Compare"
- Fifth block: "Studio critique"

The key product rule: every block should ask the learner to answer something. If the user can passively scroll without making a judgment, the block is not active learning.
