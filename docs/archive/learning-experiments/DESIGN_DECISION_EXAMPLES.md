# Design Decision Examples

Generated: 2026-06-09

Scope: design decision training examples for five selected building cases. No database changes, no taxonomy changes, no metadata changes.

Goal: test whether Archistory can move from case study to design judgment training. The aim is not to ask students to find the correct answer. The aim is to help them understand that architecture is made through trade-offs between several reasonable choices.

## How To Use This Layer

Each case presents a design decision challenge:

1. A design problem.
2. Four possible design choices.
3. The advantage of each choice.
4. The cost of each choice.
5. Why the architect's built choice matters.
6. What would change if another choice had been made.

Students should be asked to defend a position, revise a choice, or explain a trade-off. The product value is in the reasoning, not in selecting a hidden answer.

## 1. Church of the Light

Architect: Tadao Ando
Current page slug: `church-of-light`

### Design Decision Challenge

You are designing a small chapel on a limited budget. The room must feel spiritually intense without relying on decoration. How should the main sacred atmosphere be created?

### Four Possible Choices

#### Option A: Use a symbolic light opening

**Advantages**

- Creates a clear visual and spiritual focus.
- Makes the wall, section, and light work together.
- Allows a small building to feel memorable without ornament.

**Costs**

- The room may become dependent on one iconic image.
- If the light condition is weak, the concept loses force.
- The symbol may overpower more subtle spatial experiences.

#### Option B: Use traditional religious ornament

**Advantages**

- Visitors can immediately recognize sacred meaning.
- Symbolic communication is direct and familiar.
- The space may feel warmer or more culturally legible.

**Costs**

- Ornament may weaken the abstract power of the room.
- The building risks becoming conventional rather than spatially precise.
- Budget and craft requirements increase.

#### Option C: Use a dramatic roof form

**Advantages**

- The chapel gains strong external identity.
- Section and ceiling can produce spatial drama.
- The building may become more visible as a civic or religious landmark.

**Costs**

- The focus shifts from interior light to exterior form.
- Structure becomes more complex.
- The small scale may be overwhelmed by formal gesture.

#### Option D: Use landscape procession

**Advantages**

- The sacred experience begins before entering the room.
- Approach, pause, and threshold become part of the ritual.
- The building can feel less image-dependent.

**Costs**

- Requires more site control.
- The interior may feel less concentrated.
- The project becomes harder to understand from one image.

### Why The Built Choice Matters

Ando's built choice is closest to Option A, but it works only because it is supported by restraint: concrete walls, darkness, simple seating, and a controlled axis. The design does not merely add a cross. It removes competing elements so light can carry the meaning.

### If Another Choice Had Been Made

If the chapel used ornament, it would be more immediately readable but less radical as a modern sacred space. If it used a dramatic roof, it might become more object-like and less inward. If it relied on landscape procession, it would move closer to Water Temple, where the route creates meaning before the room.

## 2. Villa Savoye

Architect: Le Corbusier
Current page slug: `villa-savoye`

### Design Decision Challenge

You are designing a modern private house in a landscape. Should the house prioritize domestic comfort, theoretical clarity, movement sequence, or integration with the ground?

### Four Possible Choices

#### Option A: Lift the house and make it an object in the landscape

**Advantages**

- Creates a strong modern image.
- Separates structure from enclosure.
- Makes arrival, car movement, and entry part of the architectural sequence.

**Costs**

- The house may feel less rooted in the site.
- Everyday comfort can be secondary to conceptual clarity.
- Technical performance and maintenance may suffer.

#### Option B: Embed the house into the landscape

**Advantages**

- Creates stronger continuity between house and site.
- Outdoor living can feel more natural.
- The building may be more comfortable and less exposed.

**Costs**

- The modern manifesto becomes less legible.
- The structural idea of pilotis is weakened.
- The house may lose its abstract force.

#### Option C: Organize the house around a central courtyard

**Advantages**

- Creates privacy, light, and outdoor space.
- Makes daily life more inward and protected.
- Gives the plan a clear domestic center.

**Costs**

- The promenade becomes less linear and less cinematic.
- The building may feel more traditional.
- The roof garden and elevated object idea become less important.

#### Option D: Make the house a compact functional box

**Advantages**

- More efficient for daily living.
- Easier to build, maintain, and adapt.
- Less conflict between theory and use.

**Costs**

- The building loses its manifesto quality.
- Movement becomes ordinary.
- The Five Points become less visible as a complete argument.

### Why The Built Choice Matters

Le Corbusier's built choice is closest to Option A. Villa Savoye sacrifices some domestic ease in order to make a clear argument about modern life: pilotis, ramp, roof garden, horizontal window, and free plan become a continuous spatial manifesto.

### If Another Choice Had Been Made

If the house were embedded in the ground, it might be more comfortable but less iconic. If it used a courtyard, it would shift from modern promenade to protected domestic center. If it were a compact functional box, it might work better as a house but would teach less about modernism as an architectural argument.

## 3. Kimbell Art Museum

Architect: Louis Kahn
Current page slug: `kimbell-art-museum`

### Design Decision Challenge

You are designing an art museum. Should the galleries be neutral containers, dramatic architectural rooms, daylight-controlled spaces, or flexible black-box galleries?

### Four Possible Choices

#### Option A: Use controlled natural light as the organizing principle

**Advantages**

- Gives galleries a calm and humane atmosphere.
- Connects structure, section, and viewing experience.
- Makes the museum itself teach visitors how to see.

**Costs**

- Requires careful technical control.
- Not all artworks tolerate natural light equally.
- Flexibility may be limited by the daylight system.

#### Option B: Use fully neutral white-box galleries

**Advantages**

- Highly adaptable for many exhibition types.
- Reduces architectural interference with art.
- Easier to standardize curatorial conditions.

**Costs**

- The museum may feel generic.
- Visitors may not remember the spatial experience.
- Structure and light become less educational.

#### Option C: Use dramatic monumental galleries

**Advantages**

- Creates strong institutional identity.
- Visitors feel the public importance of the museum.
- Large works may gain powerful settings.

**Costs**

- Architecture may compete with art.
- Scale can become intimidating.
- Smaller works may be overwhelmed.

#### Option D: Use flexible black-box exhibition spaces

**Advantages**

- Supports media art, projection, and changing installations.
- Gives curators strong environmental control.
- Reduces daylight risks.

**Costs**

- Loses the daily variation of natural light.
- Can feel detached from site and time.
- Makes structure and material less visible to visitors.

### Why The Built Choice Matters

Kahn's built choice is closest to Option A. The Kimbell does not disappear as a neutral container, but it also avoids spectacle. It uses structure and daylight to create a calm framework for looking.

### If Another Choice Had Been Made

If the museum were a white box, it might be more flexible but less memorable. If it were monumental, it might compete with the art. If it were a black box, it would gain control but lose the subtle relationship between time, daylight, material, and viewing.

## 4. Rolex Learning Center

Architect: SANAA
Current page slug: `rolex-learning-center`

### Design Decision Challenge

You are designing a university learning center. Should learning be organized by rooms, corridors, open fields, or landscape-like topography?

### Four Possible Choices

#### Option A: Create a continuous topographic learning field

**Advantages**

- Encourages informal learning and social encounter.
- Blends circulation, study, meeting, and rest.
- Gives the building a distinctive spatial identity.

**Costs**

- Orientation can become difficult.
- Acoustic and privacy problems are harder to control.
- Accessibility and furniture planning become more demanding.

#### Option B: Use a conventional corridor-and-room layout

**Advantages**

- Easy to understand and manage.
- Clear separation between quiet study, group work, and services.
- Efficient for operations and wayfinding.

**Costs**

- Informal learning may be less spontaneous.
- The building may feel institutional and predictable.
- Circulation becomes leftover space rather than learning space.

#### Option C: Use a central atrium with surrounding rooms

**Advantages**

- Creates a clear social heart.
- Easier orientation than a fully open field.
- Balances openness with enclosed program areas.

**Costs**

- Activity may concentrate too much in one zone.
- The plan becomes more hierarchical.
- Peripheral rooms may feel secondary.

#### Option D: Use stacked floors with specialized zones

**Advantages**

- Allows strong acoustic and program separation.
- Easier to phase, manage, and secure.
- Different floors can support different learning modes.

**Costs**

- Reduces continuous visual connection.
- Movement becomes vertical and segmented.
- The sense of shared learning landscape is weakened.

### Why The Built Choice Matters

SANAA's built choice is closest to Option A. The project treats learning as a social and spatial field rather than a set of separated rooms. The floor itself becomes the organizing device.

### If Another Choice Had Been Made

If the building used corridors, it would be clearer but less transformative. If it used an atrium, it would gain an obvious center but lose the distributed field quality. If it used stacked zones, it would solve some functional problems but weaken the experience of continuous learning.

## 5. 21st Century Museum Kanazawa

Architect: SANAA
Current page slug: `kanazawa-museum`

### Design Decision Challenge

You are designing a public museum in the city. Should the museum have one clear front, many equal entries, a monumental civic facade, or a hidden landscape strategy?

### Four Possible Choices

#### Option A: Use a circular plan with multiple entries

**Advantages**

- Makes the museum feel open from several directions.
- Reduces institutional hierarchy.
- Encourages visitors to create their own routes.

**Costs**

- Wayfinding can become less clear.
- The museum may lack a strong ceremonial front.
- Galleries must be carefully organized within a public field.

#### Option B: Use one formal main entrance

**Advantages**

- Clear orientation and visitor control.
- Strong civic identity.
- Easier ticketing, security, and arrival sequence.

**Costs**

- The museum feels more hierarchical.
- Other sides of the building may become inactive.
- Public access becomes less distributed.

#### Option C: Use a monumental facade facing the city

**Advantages**

- Creates a strong urban symbol.
- Makes institutional importance visible.
- Supports ceremonial public presence.

**Costs**

- The museum may feel less approachable.
- The facade can dominate over gallery and public life.
- The building becomes more frontal and less open.

#### Option D: Bury the museum into landscape

**Advantages**

- Reduces visual mass.
- Can create powerful atmospheric interiors.
- Makes landscape part of the museum experience.

**Costs**

- Public access may be less immediate.
- The museum becomes less transparent as a civic institution.
- Orientation depends more on route and threshold.

### Why The Built Choice Matters

SANAA's built choice is closest to Option A. The circular plan and transparent edge make the museum behave less like a monument and more like a public field. The design decision changes how the institution meets the city.

### If Another Choice Had Been Made

If the museum had one formal entrance, it would be easier to control but less democratic. If it had a monumental facade, it would gain symbolic force but lose lightness. If it were buried into landscape, it might become atmospheric but less immediately civic.

## Product Takeaway

This layer can train students to see design as a chain of trade-offs:

- A stronger image may reduce flexibility.
- More openness may reduce clarity.
- More neutrality may reduce spatial memory.
- More symbolic force may reduce ambiguity.
- More public access may complicate control.

For Archistory, the opportunity is to let each canonical building become a decision simulator. The student does not only learn what was built. The student learns what else could have been built, why that alternative was reasonable, and what was gained or lost by the final choice.
