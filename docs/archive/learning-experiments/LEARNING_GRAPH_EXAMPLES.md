## 1. Church of the Light

Architect: Tadao Ando
Current page slug: `church-of-light`

### Core Concepts

**Daylight as architectural material**
Why it matters: daylight is not only illumination; it can organize attention, atmosphere, and meaning.
Why this building is a good entry: the cross-shaped opening makes light the main spatial event, so students can immediately see how a small daylight decision changes the whole room.

**Sacred space without ornament**
Why it matters: many students assume religious architecture depends on iconography or historical style.
Why this building is a good entry: it shows that proportion, darkness, orientation, and light can produce sacred intensity without decorative language.

**Concrete as atmosphere**
Why it matters: material is not just structure or finish; it shapes silence, weight, temperature, and mood.
Why this building is a good entry: exposed concrete creates the neutral field that allows light to become dramatic.

**Controlled visual axis**
Why it matters: where the eye moves first often reveals the building's hierarchy.
Why this building is a good entry: seating, wall, and opening work together to direct attention toward one concentrated event.

**Reduction and restraint**
Why it matters: good architecture often comes from deciding what to remove.
Why this building is a good entry: almost every element is edited down, making it easy to discuss what remains and why.

**Threshold between ordinary and sacred**
Why it matters: buildings often create meaning through transitions, not only through rooms.
Why this building is a good entry: the contrast between exterior simplicity and interior light helps students understand threshold as a design tool.

### Related Concepts

- Wall opening: important because a single opening can determine light, view, symbol, and orientation.
- Darkness: important because darkness is an active design condition, not a failure of lighting.
- Ritual sequence: important because sacred architecture often depends on approach, pause, and focus.
- Minimalism: important because visual simplicity can hide strong spatial control.
- Material honesty: important because exposed material changes how users read structure and surface.

### Related Buildings

- Water Temple: compare sacred experience through path and descent rather than a frontal image.
- Chapel of Notre Dame du Haut: compare expressive sacred form with Ando's restrained sacred space.
- MIT Chapel: compare focused light in an enclosed worship space.
- Kimbell Art Museum: compare controlled daylight in a non-religious setting.
- Row House in Sumiyoshi: compare Ando's use of concrete, absence, and controlled daily movement.

### Related Architects

- Tadao Ando: primary reference for concrete, light, restraint, and spiritual atmosphere.
- Le Corbusier: useful contrast for modern sacred space through Chapel of Notre Dame du Haut.
- Louis Kahn: useful for comparing light, silence, and monumentality.
- Eero Saarinen: useful through MIT Chapel as another modern sacred interior.

### Related Exam Topics

- Daylighting and openings: this building helps students understand how opening position and wall treatment affect interior quality.
- Interior orientation: the chapel makes visual direction easy to analyze.
- Material and finish: exposed concrete shows how material choice affects perception.
- Religious building planning: the case gives a compact example of worship space organization.
- Section and enclosure: light and wall thickness make section more than a technical drawing.

### Related Design Studio Topics

- Designing with one dominant idea.
- Editing a concept until every element supports it.
- Creating atmosphere without decoration.
- Using light as a spatial organizer.
- Defending minimal form with precise evidence.

### If This Is The Only Building The User Studies First

Best next path:

1. Study daylight as a design tool through Church of the Light.
2. Compare with Water Temple to see how Ando uses path instead of a single visual symbol.
3. Compare with Chapel of Notre Dame du Haut to understand another modern sacred strategy.
4. Move to Kimbell Art Museum to study controlled daylight in a museum.
5. Return to exam topics: openings, orientation, material expression, and religious space planning.

## 2. Villa Savoye

Current page slug: `villa-savoye`

**Architectural promenade**

**Five Points of Architecture**

**Pilotis and ground relationship**

**Horizontal window**

**Free plan**

## Overall Product Validation

These five examples show that Archistory can generate a useful learning graph from a building case if the graph is structured around learning reasons, not raw tags.

The strongest graph pattern is:

1. Building
2. Core concept
3. Why the concept matters
4. Why this building teaches it well
5. Related comparison
6. Exam or studio topic
7. Next learning path

This means a building page can become more than an archive page. It can become a guided starting point: a student opens one building, learns a concept through a concrete case, compares it with another building, and then moves toward exam knowledge or studio judgment.
